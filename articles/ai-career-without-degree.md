There is no single route into AI work without a degree, and nothing guarantees a job. What you can control is evidence. If you lack the credential, aim for public, checkable proof that you can build, work with others and finish things. Team projects and hackathon submissions are good ways to create that proof on your own schedule.

### What does a reviewer need to see?

A degree is a shortcut. It tells a reader that someone else has already checked you for a few years. Without it, the reader has to check you directly. Assume they will not spend long doing so, and make that time easy.

A useful exercise is to imagine a reviewer asking a few plain questions:

- Can this person build something that works, not just follow a tutorial?
- Do they understand what they built, including its limits?
- Can they work with other people and explain decisions?
- Do they finish things by a deadline?

Course completion on its own does not answer many of these. A working project, with code, a short demo and a clear explanation of your part in it, can answer most of them. The rest of this article is about creating that kind of proof, one project at a time.

### Why do team projects and hackathon submissions help?

Solo side projects are useful, but a reviewer cannot always tell how much was yours, how long it took or whether anyone else found it useful. Team projects and hackathon submissions add outside signals.

| Signal | What it can show a reviewer |
| --- | --- |
| A team repository with your commits | You work in shared code and communicate through it |
| A fixed deadline | You can scope and ship, not just start |
| A submission that others reviewed | Someone outside your circle looked at it seriously |
| A recorded demo or pitch | You can explain technical work to non-specialists |
| Feedback you acted on | You can take criticism and improve |

A hackathon puts several of these into one short, focused event. You build with a team against a deadline and submit your work. Whatever the result, the submission is a dated record of what you built and how you worked.

### How do you pick projects that point toward a role?

"AI career" covers many jobs. Choose projects that look like the work you want, so each one is evidence for a specific role.

1. Pick a target role. For example: AI application developer, data analyst using AI tools, AI product designer or technical writer for AI products.
2. List the tasks that role does. Read several job posts and note recurring tasks, not just tool names.
3. Build projects that include those tasks. For example, a would-be AI application developer might build an app that calls a hosted model API and handles errors. A product designer might own the user flow and test it with real users.
4. Bring your existing experience. If you are changing careers, you have knowledge of another field. A project for your old industry, built with people who know AI, can stand out more than a generic chatbot.

### How do you turn a submission into evidence?

A submission only helps if a reviewer can find it and understand it quickly. After each event, set aside an hour for this checklist:

- The repository is public, or you have permission to share it.
- The README says what the project does, who it is for and how to run it.
- A short demo video or live link is near the top.
- A "My role" section states exactly what you did, in one or two honest sentences.
- You note what you would change with more time.
- Your profile or personal site links to it, with the event name and date.

Be precise about credit. If a teammate built the model integration and you built the interface, say so. Overclaiming is easy to spot when someone reads the code, and accuracy is part of the proof.

### How do you talk about it in applications and interviews?

Lead with the project, not the missing degree. A simple structure works: the problem, what your team built, your part, one decision you made and why, and what you learned from the reactions of the people who tried it.

Be ready to talk about something that went wrong. Have one real example, such as a feature you cut to meet the deadline, and explain the trade-off. This shows judgment, which is hard to prove any other way. Keep the link to the project in your resume, your profile and your email signature, so the proof is one click away.

### What should you watch out for?

- Quantity over depth. A few complete projects you can explain in detail are stronger than many half-finished ones.
- Hiding the AI parts. If a model does the core work, show how you prompt it, check its output and handle failures. Models can produce wrong answers, and showing that you planned for this is part of the proof.
- Skipping the rules. Read each event's rules before you start and before you publish anything. If you have questions about who owns the code, ask the organisers or get proper advice.
- Expecting a fixed timeline. Treat this as a steady habit, not a sprint.
