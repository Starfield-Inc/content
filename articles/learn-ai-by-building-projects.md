To learn AI by building instead of watching, give every project three things: a specific goal, a fixed deadline and feedback from someone outside your head. Use tutorials only when you are stuck on a step in that build. A hackathon gives you all three at once, and you can use the same method at home.

### Why do tutorials stop working after a while?

Tutorials are good at showing you what is possible. They are weak at teaching you to decide. Someone else has already picked the problem, chosen the tools, fixed the errors and cut the scope. You follow along, the code runs and it feels like progress.

The gap shows up when you open an empty editor. You have to choose what to build, which model or API to call, how to structure the data and what to do when the output is wrong. None of those choices were yours in the tutorial, so you have not practiced them.

Building forces those choices. It is slower and less comfortable, and we think that discomfort is the point. You also meet the messy parts tutorials tend to leave out: unclear requirements, model output that is sometimes wrong, slow responses and data that does not match the example.

### What makes a build teach you?

An open-ended "I'll build an AI app someday" tends to drift. A build that teaches has three parts.

| Ingredient | What it does | Without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A specific goal | Tells you what "done" means | You keep adding features and never finish |
| A deadline | Forces you to cut scope and decide | The project stalls at the first hard bug |
| Outside feedback | Shows you what you cannot see yourself | You only learn what you already suspected |

A good goal is one sentence with a user in it. "An app that turns a recipe photo into a shopping list for one person" is a goal. "Something with computer vision" is a topic.

A good deadline is short and real. Give yourself a weekend or a week for a first version. Tell someone the date, so it is hard to move.

Good feedback comes from people who did not build it. That could be a friend who tries the app, someone in a community channel or the people reviewing a hackathon submission. Ask them to use it, not just look at it.

### How do you set up a learning build?

Use this plan for your next project.

1. Write the goal in one sentence. Include who it is for and what they get.
2. List what you do not know yet. For example: calling a hosted model API, storing and searching your own data, building a simple interface. This list is your learning plan.
3. Set the deadline and the demo. Decide what you will show at the end, such as a two-minute screen recording of the main flow.
4. Build the thinnest version first. Get one input to produce one useful output, end to end, even if it is ugly.
5. Look things up only when blocked. When you hit a step you cannot do, find the shortest guide or doc page for that step, apply it, then return to the build.
6. Get feedback before the deadline. Show the rough version to at least one person halfway through.
7. Write down what you learned. A short list in the README: what worked, what broke, what you would change.

### How should you use tutorials instead?

Tutorials are still useful. Change when and how you use them.

- Before a build: skim one or two to see what is possible. Do not code along.
- During a build: use them just in time, for the one step you are stuck on.
- After a build: watch or read about the part you found hardest. It will make far more sense now.

A simple test: if you finish a tutorial and could not rebuild the core part without it, you watched it. If you used it to unblock your own project, you learned from it. For just-in-time help, try official docs and short written guides first. They are quicker to scan than a long video.

### How does a hackathon give you all three?

A hackathon is a ready-made learning build. The theme helps you set a goal. The submission deadline is fixed and shared by everyone. Presenting your work to other people gives you feedback, and preparing a demo forces you to explain what you built.

You also learn from teammates. Working with someone who knows a different part of the stack shows you how they make decisions, which a tutorial cannot do. Before you join any event, read its rules and description so you know what to expect.

Here is a simple rhythm to try: one solo learning build, then one hackathon, then repeat. The solo build lets you practice a skill. The hackathon tests it under pressure, with people who will tell you what is not working.
