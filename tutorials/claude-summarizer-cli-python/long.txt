The town of Alderbrook voted last spring to replace its ageing bus fleet. The council asked three operators to bid, and the contract went to a regional company that offered electric buses on a ten-year lease. Officials said the old diesel buses averaged 17 years of age and broke down often, which led to cancelled trips on the two busiest routes.

The first six electric buses arrive in March. They will run on Route 4, which links the railway station with the hospital, and on Route 9, which crosses the town from the north estate to the market square. The company says each bus can travel about 220 kilometres on one charge, which is enough for a full day on both routes.

Charging is the part that worries residents most. The council plans a depot on the old gasworks site, and the depot will hold eight chargers. Construction needs a new connection to the power grid, and the grid operator has said the connection could take up to five months. If it is late, the buses will charge at the company's depot in the next town, which adds a 40-minute trip at each end of the day.

Drivers have been offered a two-day training course. The union says most drivers welcome the quieter vehicles but want more detail on what happens when a battery runs low in cold weather. The company answered that the buses show remaining range on a screen and that a driver can swap to a spare bus at the station. It has not published data on winter range for this model.

The lease costs the council 1.9 million pounds a year, slightly less than the current spending on diesel, repairs and fuel taken together. Council papers say the saving depends on fuel prices staying near their current level. A report by the council's finance team puts the break-even point at a diesel price about 8 percent lower than today.

Fares will not change this year. A review in the autumn will look at whether to add a cheaper off-peak fare, which a local rider group has requested for two years. The review will also look at whether Route 9 should run later on Friday and Saturday evenings, when the market square has events.

Not everyone supports the plan. A small group of councillors argued that the money would be better spent on more frequent diesel services, since a bus that runs every 10 minutes helps more people than a cleaner bus that runs every 30. The majority replied that the lease can be changed after two years if ridership does not grow.

Environmental groups welcomed the vote but asked for the numbers behind the emissions claim. The council says the new fleet will cut the town's bus emissions at the tailpipe to zero, and it has promised a full-year report on total emissions, including the power used for charging, after the first twelve months of service.

Business owners near the market square said they expect little change. A few asked about noise, because the old buses were loud enough that some shops kept their doors closed. The company says the electric buses are much quieter at low speed and that it will add a soft warning sound so that pedestrians can hear them.

The council will hold two public meetings in February. Residents can ask about the routes, the depot and the training plan. A summary of the questions and the answers will be posted on the council website within a week of each meeting, and the council has promised to update the lease terms in public if they change.
